Rental property · 2 partners

One 50/50 LLC or two separate LLCs?

How to structure a rental owned by two partners, how profit, loss and taxes flow to each person, and how it works when each partner owns through a trust.

Short answer: for most two-partner rentals, form one LLC owned 50/50 by both partners and let it be taxed as a partnership. Use a separate LLC for each additional property.

Option 1 — One LLC, 50/50 Recommended

Partner A (50%) ─┐
                 ├── Rental LLC ── owns property
Partner B (50%) ─┘

How tax works: an LLC with two members is taxed as a partnership by default. The LLC files Form 1065 each year, which is informational, so the LLC itself pays no tax. Each partner receives a K-1 showing their 50% of income, expenses and depreciation, and reports it on Schedule E of their own Form 1040. The 50/50 split of P&L and tax happens automatically.

Pros

  • One bank account, one set of books, one tax return
  • Lender, insurer and title company deal with one owner
  • Clean liability protection
  • Easy to split profit, loss and depreciation

Cons

  • CPA fee for the partnership return, often $500–$1,500+ a year
  • Partners are tied together, so a strong operating agreement is essential
Note: an LLC with two owners can't be "100% owned" and split later. Once two people own it, it is a partnership for tax purposes. The exception is a married couple in a community-property state, who can elect to be treated as a single owner.

Option 2 — Two LLCs, one per partner

There are two ways to set this up, and neither is ideal for a typical deal.

2a. Each LLC owns 50% of the property directly (tenants-in-common)

  • Lenders dislike it; refinancing and selling are harder
  • Both LLCs must sign everything
  • Mainly useful for 1031-exchange situations where partners may split up later

2b. Each partner's LLC owns 50% of one shared Rental LLC (holding structure)

Partner A → A Holding LLC (50%) ─┐
                                 ├── Rental LLC ── property
Partner B → B Holding LLC (50%) ─┘
  • Extra privacy and estate-planning flexibility; each partner manages their own share
  • Same tax result, but more formation cost and annual state fees (e.g. California charges $800 per LLC per year)
  • Only worth it if each partner owns other properties or has specific asset-protection needs

Recommended setup

  1. Form one LLC with 50/50 ownership, taxed as a partnership.
  2. Use a separate LLC for each property as you grow, so a lawsuit on one property can't reach the others.
  3. Get the operating agreement right. It matters more than the structure. It should cover:
    • Each partner's contribution, and what happens if someone can't pay their share of a capital call
    • Who decides what, and how a 50/50 deadlock is broken
    • A buy-sell clause for death, divorce, disability or a partner who wants out, including right of first refusal and how the price is set
    • How profits are split and when cash is distributed
  4. Financing: conventional loans are hard to get in an LLC's name; many investors use DSCR or commercial loans. If you buy in personal names and transfer to the LLC later, the loan's due-on-sale clause could let the lender demand full repayment, so ask the lender first.
  5. Insurance: add an umbrella policy. The LLC alone isn't full protection.

When each partner owns through a trust

This works well and is common. Each partner's trust owns their 50% share of the LLC, and the LLC owns the property.

Partner A → A's Living Trust (50%) ─┐
                                    ├── Rental LLC ── owns property
Partner B → B's Living Trust (50%) ─┘
Key point: put the LLC share in the trust, not the property itself. The LLC still holds the deed.

Revocable living trusts Most common

Tax: the same as owning the share personally.

  • A revocable trust is a "grantor trust," so the IRS ignores it for income tax
  • The LLC still files one Form 1065 and issues two K-1s, one to each trust
  • Each partner reports their 50% on their own Form 1040, Schedule E, under their own SSN
  • The trust doesn't file a separate tax return

Benefits

  • Avoids probate. If a partner dies, their successor trustee steps in right away, so rent collection, mortgage payments and decisions continue
  • Privacy. A will goes through public probate; a trust doesn't
  • Step-up in basis at death. The heirs' tax basis resets to current market value, which can wipe out capital gains tax on years of appreciation

Limits

  • No asset protection: creditors can still reach the share
  • Doesn't change the yearly tax result

Tip: after a partner's death, ask the CPA to make a Section 754 election on the partnership return so the step-up also applies to the property inside the LLC. That resets depreciation and lowers the gain on a later sale.

Irrevocable trusts

  • Tax: many are not grantor trusts. They file their own Form 1041, and trust income hits the top 37% rate at only about $15,000 of income. Paying income out to beneficiaries can avoid that.
  • Asset protection: often stronger than a revocable trust
  • Step-up in basis: often lost if the share isn't counted in the partner's estate
  • Flexibility: you generally can't take the share back out

Use one only if an estate-planning attorney recommends it for a specific reason, such as estate tax or asset protection.

What the operating agreement must cover with trusts

  1. Trusts as members. Name each trust as the member, or allow transfers to a partner's own trust without the other partner's consent.
  2. Who votes. The trustee acts for the trust: usually the partner while alive, then the successor trustee.
  3. Death or incapacity. Does the successor trustee or beneficiary become a full voting member, or only receive money?
  4. Buy-sell. Can the surviving partner buy out the deceased partner's trust? At what price and on what payment terms? Life insurance on each partner is a common way to fund this.
  5. Unwanted co-owners. Without these rules, you could end up 50/50 with your partner's heirs or ex-spouse.

Setup steps

  1. Form the LLC, ideally with each trust named as a member from the start.
  2. If the LLC already exists, each partner signs an assignment of membership interest to their trust. Update the LLC's member records and the bank's records.
  3. Confirm the lender allows it. Most are fine with transfers to revocable trusts, but check first.
  4. Keep the property title in the LLC's name, and update insurance to list the trusts and the LLC as insureds.
Spouses: if the two partners are married to each other and share a joint revocable trust, the LLC could have just one member, depending on the state and setup. That can remove the partnership tax return entirely. Ask a CPA.

When two LLCs could make sense

  • The partners have very different goals, such as one wanting to sell soon and the other to 1031-exchange
  • One partner faces higher personal liability risk (for example, a doctor)
  • Each partner already has a holding LLC for other properties
General information only, not legal or tax advice. LLC fees and rules vary by state. Have a local real-estate attorney draft the operating agreement and a CPA set up the partnership return. If trusts are involved, have the attorney who drafted each trust review the operating agreement so the documents match.
